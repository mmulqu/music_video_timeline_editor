# Abstract narrative language

Use this guide to make a sequence tell a story without literal illustration.

## State channels

Track four channels across every shot:

1. **Motif** — a persistent form that can recur, multiply, disappear, or return.
2. **Geometry** — order, recursion, density, fracture, saturation, residue.
3. **Material** — paper, mineral, film, pigment, signal, emulsion, sediment.
4. **Palette** — controlled color roles that change with the narrative.

Add a fifth channel, **tempo**, through shot duration, motion, and transition.

## Prompt order

Lead with the image's ontology before its subject:

1. `EDGE-TO-EDGE NONREPRESENTATIONAL FIELD EXTENDING BEYOND EVERY IMAGE EDGE`
2. composition and negative space
3. geometry
4. material
5. palette
6. motif state
7. surface damage or atmosphere
8. exclusions

This reduces literal landscape drift.

Avoid using `canvas`, `painting on a wall`, or similar object language when the
desired output is the image itself. Models may literalize those terms as a
framed artwork, diptych, book, mat, or gallery mockup. When generating a
full-frame abstraction, add negatives for `gallery mockup`, `picture frame`,
`border`, `mat`, `canvas object`, `artwork on wall`, and `white margin`.

## Continuity rules

- Change only one or two dominant state channels between adjacent shots.
- Carry one recognizable motif across act boundaries.
- Reserve the largest palette or density discontinuity for the narrative peak.
- Let the aftermath reuse an early motif in an altered state.
- Use repetition with mutation, not unrelated novelty.

## Useful transformation verbs

Accumulate, index, compress, align, copy, recurse, leak, fracture, eclipse,
saturate, erase, fossilize, sediment, reappear.

## Literal exclusions

For technological or speculative stories, usually exclude:

- humanoid robots
- brains, faces, eyes, and hands
- computer screens and server racks
- circuit-board illustration
- skylines and literal cities
- mushroom clouds and disaster spectacle
- readable typography, logos, and diagrams that look instructional

Express causality through changing systems, scale, material, and rhythm.

## Music correspondence

Map visual state to music without scoring every cut:

- geometry density → event density
- palette brightness → spectral brightness
- recursion → repeating cells and phase relationships
- fracture → displaced rhythm and discontinuity
- emptiness → sparse register and long decay
- aftermath → stretched or incomplete return of an earlier motif

Always request instrumental output with no vocals, spoken word, or choir.
